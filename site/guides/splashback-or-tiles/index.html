Glass splashback or tiles?

An honest comparison, including when tiles are the better answer.

Guide462 words

Where glass wins

A single pane behind a cooktop has no grout, which is the thing that discolours, cracks and collects grease. It is wiped in one movement, it reflects light back into a bench run, and it can be cut around power outlets so there are no awkward cuts at the worst place on the wall.

Glass also suits a kitchen where the cabinetry is the feature, because a plain sheet does not compete with it the way a patterned tile does.

Where tiles win

If the wall is very out of square, tile follows it and glass does not — a large pane on a wavy wall shows every deviation. Tiles are also easier to change in part later, and cheaper where the area is small and awkward.

A tiled splashback can also be replaced by a tiler without touching the kitchen; a glass one has to be measured, made and fitted, so it is a bigger commitment.

The practical details people forget

Glass needs a gap from the cooktop, and the size of that gap depends on the appliance, not on taste. It also needs somewhere for the edge to land: a bench that meets the wall cleanly on one side and a cupboard on the other is easy; a raw plasterboard edge is not.

The cut-outs are the other thing. Every power point, every tap, every range hood fixing is a hole placed from a site measurement, and once the glass is toughened none of them can move.

Choose on the wall you have

The right question is not which looks better in a photo. It is whether your wall is flat enough for a pane, whether the bench and cupboards give the glass a clean edge to sit against, and whether you want to change it again in five years.

If the answer to those is yes, glass is the lower-maintenance choice. If the wall is a mess or the budget is tight, tile is not a compromise — it is the correct answer.

In short
  • Glass: no grout, easy clean, light into the bench — needs a flat wall
  • Tile: follows an out-of-square wall and is cheaper to change in part
  • Appliance clearance decides part of the layout before anything is ordered
  • Every cut-out is measured once and cannot be moved after tempering

QuestionsGlass splashback or tiles?
Can a splashback go behind a freestanding cooker?
Yes, with the correct clearance from the appliance and a suitable make-up. The appliance's installation instructions set that distance, so bring them to the measure.
Can you cut around existing power points?
Yes — the cut-outs are measured from the installed positions. That is also why we measure after the electrician has finished, not before.
Will a large pane show the wall behind it?
It will show the wall it is fixed to. That is the main argument for tile in an older kitchen.
