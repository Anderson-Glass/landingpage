Framed, frameless or demountable

Three ways to divide a floor, and the questions that decide between them.

Guide555 words

Start with what the room has to do

A meeting room needs acoustic separation and a door that closes properly. A finance team needs privacy and a place to put a lock. A corridor needs light without turning into a tunnel. Those three briefs produce three different partition details, even when the drawings look similar.

So the useful first question is not "framed or frameless" — it is what happens in the room, who else is on the floor, and what the ceiling and floor can carry.

Framed: the default for a reason

Framed aluminium partitions carry their structure in the frame, which means the glass can be thinner, the panels can be larger, and doors hang from something designed to take the weight. They also give you a place to put a head channel that lands on structure rather than on plasterboard, and a base detail that survives a floor that is not level.

Most commercial fitouts end up here because it is the cheapest way to get a full-height, acoustically sensible wall that will still be straight in five years.

Frameless: less metal, more discipline

Frameless glazing puts the structure into the glass and the fittings, which looks better and forgives less. It needs a floor and ceiling that are actually parallel, and it needs the door hardware to be right, because there is no frame to hide a hinge in.

It also costs more per square metre than framed glazing, not because the glass is special but because the setting out and the fixings are unforgiving. Worth it where the sight lines matter; wasteful in a back-of-house corridor.

Demountable: for tenancies that move

A demountable system is designed to be taken down and rebuilt, which changes the detail everywhere: how panels lock together, how the head is trimmed, how services are handled. If a tenancy is a three-year proposition, demountable can be the difference between an asset and a skip. If it is a ten-year home, it usually buys nothing.

The details that decide whether it feels right

Manifestation — the markings on the glass — is not decoration. It is what stops people walking into a panel that looks like an opening, and it is required on large glazed areas. Where it goes and how much of it there is changes how the room feels as much as how safe it is.

The other one is the door. A glass partition with a badly hung door reads as cheap no matter how good the glass is: closers that drift, gaps at the head, and a latch that needs a shove.

In short
  • Decide from the brief (acoustics, privacy, light), not from a picture
  • Framed is cheaper and straighter; frameless is quieter visually and unforgiving
  • Demountable pays only if the tenancy is likely to move
  • Manifestation is a safety requirement that also sets the look of the room

QuestionsFramed, frameless or demountable
Can you match an existing partition?
Usually. Send a photo of the head, base and door detail and we can tell you what system it looks like before anyone visits.
How acoustic can a glass partition be?
The glass is rarely the weak point — the frame, the seals and how the head meets the ceiling do most of the work. If acoustics matter, say so at the brief stage, not after fabrication.
Do partitions need building approval?
Sometimes, depending on the building, the tenancy and whether the work touches fire-rated elements. That is a question for the building surveyor; we flag it early where it looks relevant.
